What Causes Black Streaks on Your Roof? (And How to Remove Them Safely)

By Jason Caldwell, Clean Slate Pressure Washing Published April 1, 2026 7 min read

In This Guide

  1. What Is Gloeocapsa Magma?
  2. Why Florida Roofs Are Especially Vulnerable
  3. Is Gloeocapsa Magma Dangerous?
  4. How to Safely Remove Black Streaks
  5. Prevention After Cleaning

The dark black streaks visible on most Florida roofs are caused by Gloeocapsa magma, a photosynthetic cyanobacteria (often incorrectly called algae or mold) that feeds on the limestone filler in asphalt shingles. Gloeocapsa magma produces a dark pigmented outer sheath to protect itself from ultraviolet radiation, and it is this protective sheath — not the organism itself — that creates the visible black discoloration homeowners see from the ground. The organism spreads across roof surfaces via airborne spores, which is why neighboring homes in Florida subdivisions often develop dark streaks within the same 1–2 year window. Gloeocapsa magma cannot be removed by pressure washing — it requires a sodium hypochlorite-based soft wash solution applied at under 100 PSI that kills the organism at its root without damaging shingle granules.

What Is Gloeocapsa Magma?

Gloeocapsa magma is not algae and not mold. It is a cyanobacterium — a single-celled, photosynthetic microorganism that is technically a bacterium but behaves more like a plant, producing energy through photosynthesis. Cyanobacteria are among the oldest life forms on earth, and Gloeocapsa magma in particular is exceptionally well-adapted to surviving on roof surfaces. The organism produces a thick, dark-pigmented gelatinous sheath around each cell that serves as UV protection. This dark sheath is what homeowners actually see — the cells themselves are microscopic, but the colonies and their pigmented protective layer build up into the characteristic black streaks visible from the curb.

Gloeocapsa magma feeds primarily on calcium carbonate (limestone), which manufacturers include as a filler in asphalt shingles to add weight and improve dimensional stability. The organism's chemical breakdown of the limestone weakens the granule bed over time, accelerating shingle deterioration. Reproduction happens in two ways: vegetative division (cells split and the colony expands across the roof surface) and spore production (specialized cells become airborne and travel on wind currents to colonize new roofs). This airborne dispersal is why Gloeocapsa magma tends to appear on neighboring homes within the same 1–2 year window — once a colony establishes on one roof, the entire downwind subdivision becomes vulnerable.

Why Florida Roofs Are Especially Vulnerable

Gloeocapsa magma thrives in specific environmental conditions: relative humidity above 70%, temperatures above 75°F, and sufficient (but not excessive) UV exposure. Tampa Bay's default climate — 74–78% relative humidity year-round, average temperatures of 73°F in winter and 83°F in summer, and abundant rainfall (50+ inches per year) — meets all three conditions for nearly all twelve months. Northern states with cold winters experience natural die-back periods that limit Gloeocapsa colonization. Florida does not.

Within Florida, certain roof characteristics dramatically accelerate colonization. North-facing slopes receive less direct UV than south-facing slopes and develop streaks first, often years before the south-facing side shows any visible staining. Tree canopy shade over a roofline produces the same effect — UV is the only natural inhibitor of Gloeocapsa growth, and any obstruction (oaks, palms, neighboring two-story homes) removes it. Proximity to water bodies (Alafia River corridor, Hillsborough River watershed, FishHawk Creek, Lake Brandon) increases ambient humidity locally and accelerates colonization. Shingle composition also matters: shingles with higher limestone filler content are more attractive food sources, while some newer "algae-resistant" shingles include copper-impregnated granules that release galvanic ions to inhibit growth. Homes built before approximately 2010 typically have non-algae-resistant shingles.

Is Gloeocapsa Magma Dangerous?

Gloeocapsa magma is not directly harmful to humans. It is not a respiratory hazard, does not cause structural damage from below, and does not penetrate into living spaces. The danger is to the roof itself and, indirectly, to the homeowner's wallet. The organism feeds on the limestone filler in asphalt shingles, gradually weakening the granule bed that protects the underlying asphalt from UV radiation. As the granule bed weakens, granules begin to release faster than normal — homeowners notice this as accumulated granule material in their gutters. Once granule loss accelerates, the asphalt substrate begins to oxidize and crack under continued UV exposure, leading to premature shingle failure.

There are also secondary consequences. The dark sheath that makes Gloeocapsa visible also absorbs significantly more solar heat than a clean shingle, raising attic temperatures and increasing cooling costs by an estimated 4–15% on heavily affected roofs. Florida homeowner's insurance carriers, including Citizens, have increasingly scrutinized roof condition during renewal underwriting, and roofs with severe visible deterioration may face coverage limitations or non-renewal. HOAs throughout Hillsborough County — particularly FishHawk Ranch, Panther Trace, and Heritage Isles — regularly cite Gloeocapsa streaks in exterior maintenance violation notices. See our HOA exterior cleaning guide for how to handle violation notices.

How to Safely Remove Black Streaks

Gloeocapsa magma removal requires chemistry, not force. The industry-standard treatment is a soft wash solution of sodium hypochlorite (the active ingredient in household bleach) diluted to 1–3% concentration with a surfactant additive that helps the solution cling to vertical surfaces and penetrate the cellular layer of the organism. The solution is applied to the roof at under 100 PSI using a low-pressure soft wash pump and dedicated spray tips, then allowed to dwell for 15–20 minutes. During the dwell time, the sodium hypochlorite kills the organism at the cellular level — not just the surface staining, but the cells embedded in the shingle granules. After dwell, the roof is rinsed gently with low-pressure water to remove the dead organic material and the cleaning solution.

Two common DIY approaches fail consistently. The first is pressure washing — high-pressure water blasts away the visible dark sheath, making the roof look clean for a few weeks, but the live organism remains embedded in the granule bed and rebuilds the visible staining within 3–6 months. The same pressure also strips protective shingle granules, voids manufacturer warranties, and accelerates roof failure. The second is the "bleach in a garden hose" approach, which fails because the concentration is uncontrolled (often too weak to kill the organism, sometimes strong enough to damage landscaping), the solution lacks a surfactant so it runs off vertical surfaces before doing any work, and the application is uneven, producing inconsistent results. Professional roof soft washing at Clean Slate PW uses calibrated solution concentrations, proper application equipment, and full landscaping protection protocols.

Prevention After Cleaning

No prevention method is permanent in Florida's climate, but several measures meaningfully extend the time between professional cleanings. The most effective is a growth-inhibiting post-treatment applied during the soft wash service — a low-concentration residual solution that remains on the shingle surface after the cleaning solution is rinsed off, suppressing new colonization for 12–18 months beyond the cleaning itself. This is a standard part of our roof soft washing service.

Longer-term, zinc or copper strip installation along the roof ridge line creates a continuous galvanic effect — every time it rains, dissolved zinc or copper ions wash down the roof slope, inhibiting Gloeocapsa colonization in the streak path. Strips typically install for $200–$500 per linear edge during a roof replacement or as a standalone retrofit and provide ongoing protection for the life of the strip (typically 10–15 years). Tree trimming to increase UV exposure on north-facing slopes is the third option, though most homeowners are unwilling to remove mature shade trees for cosmetic roof reasons. The realistic expectation in Florida is a 2–3 year cleaning cycle even with all preventive measures in place — see our Florida roof cleaning frequency guide for property-specific recommendations.
