1.1 hreflang Implementation
hreflang tags tell Google that / (English) and /es (Spanish) are alternate language versions of the same page. Both pages must carry BOTH hreflang tags:

On the English homepage (/):
<link rel="alternate" hreflang="en" href="https://coolcozyac.com/" />
<link rel="alternate" hreflang="es" href="https://coolcozyac.com/es" />
<link rel="alternate" hreflang="x-default" href="https://coolcozyac.com/" />

On the Spanish homepage (/es):
<link rel="alternate" hreflang="en" href="https://coolcozyac.com/" />
<link rel="alternate" hreflang="es" href="https://coolcozyac.com/es" />
<link rel="alternate" hreflang="x-default" href="https://coolcozyac.com/" />

DEV ACTION: Both the English homepage AND the Spanish homepage must carry all three hreflang tags. If either is missing, Google ignores the signal. The x-default points to the English version as the fallback for unlisted languages.
hreflang tags go in the <head> of each page. They can alternatively be specified in the XML sitemap, but <head> implementation is more reliable and easier to validate.
1.2 This Is NOT a Translation
The Spanish homepage is a culturally adapted version of the English homepage, not a word-for-word translation. Key differences:
Copy is written for a Spanish-speaking audience, not translated by Google Translate or any machine tool
The page is shorter than the English homepage — it covers services, trust signals, and contact information without replicating every section
Cultural tone is warm and personal — Spanish-language service marketing in the U.S. emphasizes family, trust, and personal relationship more directly than English equivalents
The “Se habla español” messaging on English pages is replaced here by an implicit understanding: this entire page speaks your language
The page links to the English homepage via the language toggle (EN | ES) but does NOT link to individual English service pages — if Spanish service pages are built in Phase 2, those links will be added
DEV ACTION: CRITICAL: All Spanish content in this guide must be reviewed by a native Spanish speaker before launch. The copy below is written by a proficient but non-native source. Phrasing, idiom, and regional preferences (Caribbean/Central American Spanish common in Jacksonville vs. Mexican Spanish) should be verified by someone who speaks the dialect of the target community.

3. Content Adaptation Philosophy
The Spanish homepage serves a specific audience: Spanish-speaking homeowners, renters, and property managers in Jacksonville who prefer to receive information and service in Spanish. This audience’s needs are identical to English-speaking customers (their AC is broken, they need maintenance, they want a trustworthy contractor), but the communication approach has important nuances:

Lead with trust and family — Hispanic home services marketing research consistently shows that trust, family reputation, and personal recommendation carry more weight than certifications or corporate messaging. The Spanish page leads with “familia” and “confianza” before “licencia” and “BouldZoom.”
Direct, warm tone — Use “usted” (formal you) for respect but keep the language warm and approachable, not stiff or corporate. The goal is “trusted neighbor,” not “corporate entity.”
Phone number prominence — Spanish-speaking customers in the home services market disproportionately prefer phone calls over online forms. The phone number should be even MORE prominent on this page than on the English homepage.
Avoid Spanglish — Do not mix English and Spanish within sentences. Technical HVAC terms that have common Spanish equivalents should use the Spanish term (aire acondicionado, not “AC”; calefacción, not “heating”). The brand name “Cool & Cozy AC Repair” stays in English as it is the legal business name.
Address the bilingual reality — Many Jacksonville Hispanic households are bilingual. The page should acknowledge that Cool & Cozy serves in both languages, so a Spanish-speaking homeowner can be confident that their English-speaking family members or landlord can also communicate with the company.
The Spanish copy below is written by a proficient non-native speaker and MUST be reviewed by a native speaker familiar with the Jacksonville Hispanic community’s dialect preferences before launch. Caribbean, Central American, and Mexican Spanish have vocabulary and phrasing differences that matter in marketing.
